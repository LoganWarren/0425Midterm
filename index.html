Code and Responsibility

By Logan Warren

1970s: Birth of Internet Networks

The start of network systems like ARPANET, was the groundwork for today's internet. These early networks laid the foundation for global connectivity. When programmers began developing these systems, questions around privacy and surveillance began to emerge. This was the start of where ethical considerations began to rise about data protection and user autonomy.

1970s

Foucault’s Panopticism

Foucault’s concept of the Panopticon is applicable here as it reflects the silent, observing power of early network designs. Coders began to realize that these networks allowed for a level of control or observation over users. This control created a new ethical responsibility to protect user autonomy and establish clear boundaries between observation and privacy. Foucault’s ideas work well with how programmers, even in these early stages, held the power to influence behavior and therefore have a responsibility to design ethically.

1980s: Rise of Personal Computing

When personal computers began entering homes, technology became more integrated into daily life. This was an entire new layer of responsibility for programmers. Balancing accessibility with security became essential with the new integrations that technology had. User data security became a primary ethical concern as programmers grappled with how much access they should allow applications to have.

1980s

Lee & Cook’s Internet Surveillance

Lee & Cook’s analysis of internet surveillance revealed how technology can empower users but also control them at the same time. With the spread of personal computing, opportunities for data capture grew, moving programmers toward ethical accountability to protect their users data and privacy. Their work held a high responsibility for the importance of user autonomy and the ethical implications of programming choices.

1990s: Data Tracking (Cookies)

The start of the use of cookies allowed for persistent tracking of online user behavior. Initially a tool for convenience, cookies soon sparked debates around user consent and transparency. Programmers faced the ethical dilemma of balancing functionality with user privacy, as cookies could track behavior in ways users might not anticipate or fully understand.

1990s

Paul Ford’s "What is Code?"

Ford’s perspective on code as an invisible and powerful force that holds the ethical burden of designing technologies such as cookies. This helps reveal how programmers, by designing features like cookies, shaped users online experiences in subtle ways. The introduction of cookies marked a shift towards transparency in programming ethics. Developers became more conscious of their influence on user privacy and consent as the cookies they created became a way for subtle tracking to take place.

2000s: Social Media Algorithms

When social media platforms began popping up more, algorithms began to control what we saw. They shaped public opinion and engagement. Ethical concerns arose as programmers found themselves influencing users perceptions. This was a large problem as it was often without users explicit consent or understanding. It was a balance between curating content and respecting users rights, and this balance became central to the idea of ethical programming.

2000s

LinkedIn AI Data Usage

The LinkedIn case goes to show how algorithms influence perception and behavior without transparency. As with social media algorithms, coders choices impact what information users consume, raising an ethical question around misinformation or bias. This highlights the importance of a programmers responsibility in preventing harm through balanced and fair algorithms that also respect the users

2010s: IoT Devices & Data Privacy

Internet of Things devices expanded data collection into private spaces, tracking everything from personal habits to household routines. Programmers are now tasked with embedding privacy protections into IoT devices, as ethical concerns around constant data capture became increasingly prevalent inside peoples personal spaces.

2010s

Ford’s "What is Code?"

Ford use of the invisible power coders have works here in that it shape environments, particularly through IoT devices that seamlessly blend into daily life. This embedded surveillance requires ethical programming practices. Coders now must design systems that protect privacy by default, ensuring users retain control over how their data is used and shared, especially in private environments such as ones home.

2020s: AI Ethics & Data Privacy

The integration of AI in decision making processes brought many ethical concerns, such as bias and accountability being the two main ones. AI’s ability to analyze vast amounts of data intensified the responsibility for coders to ensure these systems are fair, unbiased, and secure, as AI now influences everything from personal recommendations to hiring practices.

2020s

LinkedIn AI Data Usage

The LinkedIn article I found about AI-driven data usage demonstrates the importance of transparency and consent. The ethical dilemma here was pretty clear, users unknowingly provide data that trained AI. The people who programmed this had the responsibility to maintain transparency in AI applications and respect user consent. This was an example that should remind programmers that ethical AI deployment or any program is essential to user trust.

2020s: Biometric Data in Consumer Tech

The use of biometric data, like facial recognition and fingerprint scans, in everyday devices began to spark serious debates about data sensitivity and user consent. With data so personal, programmers faced heightened ethical considerations around securing this information and respecting user autonomy.

2020s

Lee & Cook’s Internet Surveillance

Biometric data represents an inescapable form of digital engagement. Biometrics align with Lee & Cook’s insights into surveillance expectations for Gen Y. Biometrics is another way of collecting our data that is getting harder to opt out of. Most devices, banking apps, or public transports are beginning to migrate to Biometrics for authentication or to simply use the applications of technology. Having to opt out of these digital surveillances in today’s world leads would lead us to social exclusion.

2024: Generative AI & Content Authenticity

Generative AI, capable of creating realistic text, images, and videos, raises ethical issues around misinformation and public trust. With potential misuse evident, programmers are responsible for ensuring content authenticity and embedding safeguards to prevent deception. We especially see this now as generative AI becomes more mainstream.

2024

Foucault’s Panopticism

Generative AI goes with the Panopticon’s influence by controlling the narrative and blurring the lines of authenticity. The ideas presented by Foucault’s highlights the ethical weight on programmers to preserve trust in digital media. Their code increasingly shapes public perception and truth. Programmers must be vigilant to prevent misuse and maintain the integrity, respecting the trust users place in technology.

Research Question

What amount of responsibility does a programmer hold for the ethical implications of how code affects human interactions with technology?
